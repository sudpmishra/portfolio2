A few years ago I inherited a landing page that had, and I counted, eleven different animations firing on load. Cards flew in from three directions. Text characters staggered in one at a time like a movie title sequence. A hero image did a slow zoom that never stopped. It looked, in the literal sense, impressive. It also had a bounce rate that made the founder ask me, not unkindly, "did we break something?"

We hadn't broken anything. We'd just confused motion with delight, which are not the same thing.

## Motion is a hint, not a performance

The best explanation I've found for why some animation feels great and some feels like clutter: good motion answers a question the user already has. Where did my click go? What just changed? Is this still loading, or did it break? Bad motion asks a question nobody had: why is this card doing a backflip?

Once I started auditing animation that way, most of the eleven-animation page didn't survive. The staggered text was cut first, it added four seconds before anyone could read the actual headline. The zooming hero stayed, but I made it stop after one pass instead of looping forever, because a background that never settles quietly tells your eyes to stay alert, which is exhausting on page two of a long session.

## Where motion actually earns its keep

After stripping that page down, three categories of animation kept doing real work, and they're the ones I now reach for by default:

- **Onboarding that reveals one step at a time.** Show the whole form and people freeze. Reveal fields as they're completed and people keep moving, because the interface is visibly rewarding progress.
- **Hover and preview states.** A subtle scale or shadow on a card before a click tells someone "yes, this is clickable" without a single word of copy.
- **Transitions that preserve where you are.** When a modal opens by growing out of the button that triggered it, instead of just appearing, users don't lose their spatial sense of the page. Small thing, but it's the difference between "oh, there it is" and "wait, what happened."

## The metric that mattered

We didn't track "engagement" as one fuzzy number. We tracked time to first meaningful action, meaning the first click, scroll, or input that showed someone was actually doing the thing the page existed for. Before the motion cleanup, that number was inflated by people watching the intro animation finish. After the cleanup, it dropped, in a good way, because there was nothing left to sit through.

Scroll depth on the pricing page went up slightly. Not because motion made scrolling more fun, but because we added a scroll-linked cue, a thin progress line at the top, that quietly told people how much page was left. Turns out uncertainty about "how much more is there" was quietly pushing people to bail early. Motion fixed a *information* problem, not a *boredom* problem.

## What I'd tell someone about to add animation

Ask what the animation is explaining. If the honest answer is "nothing, it just looks cool," that's fine for a portfolio piece, but it's a liability on anything with a conversion goal. If the answer is "it tells the user their action registered" or "it shows what happened without them re-reading the page," keep it, and keep it fast. Under 300ms for anything that responds to a click. People notice slow feedback as lag, not as polish.

And always, always test with reduced motion turned on. Not as an accessibility checkbox exercise, though it is that too, but because it's a useful gut check: if your page feels broken with animation off, the motion was doing load-bearing work it shouldn't have been doing. Functionality and clarity should survive without the animation. The animation should just make getting there nicer.

_The page eventually shipped with two animations instead of eleven. Nobody asked if we'd broken something._
