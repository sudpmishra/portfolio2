I still remember the first time someone told me my job wouldn't exist in five years. It was 2015, and the phrase of the moment was "no-code." I nodded politely, went back to my desk, and got a support ticket an hour later from someone whose no-code app had quietly stopped working the moment they tried to do anything more interesting than a contact form. Developers survived that one mostly by rolling their eyes and cleaning up the mess afterward.

Now it's AI's turn to be the extinction event. Except this time, the tool doing the "replacing" was, somewhat awkwardly, built by developers. There's a certain poetry in an entire profession spending its nights and weekends constructing the very thing that LinkedIn thought-leaders insist will make it redundant. It's a bit like a group of people building a fire escape and then getting nervous about the smoke.

![Two people looking at code on a laptop screen together](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1690383923011-c63f619f6a5d?auto=format&fit=crop&w=1400&q=80)

## The job description nobody actually has

If you read enough breathless think-pieces, the modern developer's job is now to sit back, sip coffee, and "orchestrate" AI agents while the machines do the actual thinking. I want that job. I have not found it yet.

What's actually happening is quieter and a little funnier: developers haven't become obsolete, they've become **translators, babysitters, and professional skeptics**, three jobs an AI is uniquely bad at doing for itself.

- **Translator**, because someone still has to turn "make it pop" from a stakeholder into an actual technical requirement, and no model has learned to read minds yet.
- **Babysitter**, because AI-generated code has the confidence of someone who's never been wrong, right up until the moment it's very wrong.
- **Professional skeptic**, because someone has to be the person asking "does this actually work, or does it just look like it works in the demo?"

I've been that person more times this year than in the previous five combined.

## The productivity illusion

AI coding assistants are, genuinely, remarkable. I've watched one scaffold a working REST API faster than I could finish my coffee. I've also watched the same tool, with the exact same tone of unwavering confidence, invent a library that doesn't exist and cite a function signature from some parallel universe where it apparently does.

That's created a new and slightly unglamorous skill requirement: **reviewing code faster than you could have written it yourself.** You're a code critic now, except instead of gallery invitations you get Slack messages titled "prod is down."

The productivity gain is real. It's just not the "zero effort, 10x engineer" gain from the product demos. It's closer to this: you write less boilerplate, and you spend that saved time reading generated code with the same suspicion a customs officer gives a suitcase that's ticking.

## What it's actually good at

To be fair, AI has quietly become excellent at a handful of things I don't miss doing by hand:

1. **Boilerplate and scaffolding.** Nobody misses writing another CRUD controller from scratch.
2. **Explaining unfamiliar code.** "What does this regex do" used to mean either deep meditation or tracking down whoever wrote it.
3. **Rubber-ducking at 11 PM.** It doesn't judge you for forgetting what a segfault is. Your coworkers might.
4. **First drafts of tests, docs, and commit messages.** No more commits that just say "fix."

What it's still not good at is understanding your specific, half-documented legacy system, built by three people who left the company, one of whom may or may not have been some kind of wizard. That system lives in your head. Your head isn't an API yet.

## The real shift: from typing to judgment

Here's the part I actually believe, past the jokes. A developer's value was never really about typing speed. It just looked that way because typing was the visible output. The real value has always been **judgment**: knowing what to build, what not to build, what breaks under load, and what the client actually meant by "make it more intuitive."

AI is extraordinarily good at generating options. It's not good at knowing which option matters, why the business needs it, or what happens when ten thousand people hit the same button during a launch while the founder is on stage. That's still a human job, for as long as software has to work in the real world instead of a nicely lit demo.

## So, are we doomed?

Only in the way photographers were "doomed" by camera phones, or accountants by spreadsheets. The floor rose. The bar moved. People who ignored the new tool got left behind; people who used it well got noticeably more effective.

The developers thriving right now aren't the ones who prompt the fastest. They're the ones who still understand *why* the code works, can spot when the AI has confidently wandered off a cliff, and hold the kind of context no model fits in its window, things like "the client will panic if that button moves" and "finance will call you personally if the invoice numbers are off by one."

## Closing thought

AI didn't come for developers' jobs. It came for the busywork, and left behind something a little more serious: **responsibility, at scale, faster.** Mistakes ship quicker now. Shortcuts compound sooner. "It probably works" gets tested in production at unprecedented speed.

So no, you're not obsolete. You've just been quietly promoted to reviewing an increasingly confident machine's homework: no extra pay, no new title, and a pull request queue that never quite empties.

_**Welcome to the future. Please review it carefully before merging.**_
